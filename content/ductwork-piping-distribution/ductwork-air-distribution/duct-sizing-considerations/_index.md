Duct sizing establishes the physical dimensions required to convey specified airflow rates while maintaining acceptable velocity, pressure drop, and acoustic performance. Systematic sizing methodology balances competing objectives including first cost, operating cost, space utilization, and noise control.

## Aspect Ratio Limitations

Aspect ratio defines the relationship between duct width and height dimensions in rectangular configurations, directly impacting structural performance, pressure drop characteristics, and installation efficiency. The ratio equals the longer dimension divided by the shorter dimension.

### Maximum Aspect Ratio Constraints

Maximum aspect ratio typically limits to 4:1 for most commercial HVAC applications, balancing fabrication economy against structural and aerodynamic performance. Ratios exceeding this limit require increased gauge thickness, closer reinforcement spacing, or supplemental stiffening to prevent excessive deflection.

High aspect ratio ducts exhibit greater surface area per unit cross-sectional area compared to square or low aspect ratio configurations. This increased surface area elevates both material costs and pressure drop due to additional wetted perimeter exposed to frictional resistance.

Structural deflection increases substantially as aspect ratios exceed 3:1, particularly under negative pressure conditions. The longer duct dimension experiences cantilever loading between reinforcement members, creating deflection that scales with the fourth power of span length.

Fabrication complexity increases with extreme aspect ratios, requiring specialized forming equipment and elevated labor input. Standard shop equipment typically accommodates ratios up to 4:1 without difficulty, while higher ratios may necessitate custom fabrication approaches.

## Rectangular Duct Dimensions

Rectangular duct sizing addresses space constraints common in building construction, fitting ductwork into limited vertical or horizontal clearances. Standard dimension increments typically progress in 1 or 2-inch steps, with larger ducts increasing by 3 or 4-inch increments.

Common residential sizes range from 4x8 inches for small branch runs to 16x24 inches for trunk applications. Commercial installations utilize substantially larger dimensions, commonly reaching 48x96 inches or greater for major distribution mains.

Coordination with structural systems requires consideration of beam depths, joist spacing, and ceiling cavity dimensions. Duct depth typically represents the constraining dimension, with width adjusted to achieve required cross-sectional area within available vertical clearance.

## Round Duct Advantages and Applications

Round duct geometry provides superior structural efficiency compared to rectangular configurations, requiring less material and reinforcement for equivalent pressure capacity. The circular cross-section distributes pressure forces uniformly, eliminating flat panel deflection concerns.

Pressure drop in round ducts decreases approximately 10 to 30 percent compared to rectangular ducts of equivalent cross-sectional area, depending on aspect ratio. This reduction stems from minimized wetted perimeter and absence of corner flow separation.

Standard round duct diameters progress in 1 or 2-inch increments, typically ranging from 4 to 72 inches or larger for special applications. Spiral lockseam construction economically produces sizes from 4 to 60 inches with consistent quality and performance.

Space utilization efficiency favors rectangular ducts in constrained vertical clearances, though round ducts prove advantageous where three-dimensional space permits. Modern building design increasingly accommodates round ductwork due to recognized performance and cost benefits.

## Equivalent Diameter Calculations

Equivalent diameter enables comparison between round and rectangular duct geometries, establishing circular dimensions that produce equivalent pressure drop characteristics. Multiple equivalency methods address different performance criteria.

The most common equivalent diameter calculation for equal friction employs the equation: D = 1.30 × [(a × b)^0.625] / [(a + b)^0.25], where D equals equivalent diameter, and a and b represent rectangular duct dimensions. This relationship ensures equal pressure drop per unit length at equivalent airflow.

Equivalent diameter for equal velocity utilizes the simpler area-based calculation: D = 1.128 × √(a × b), producing a circular diameter with cross-sectional area matching the rectangular duct. This approach maintains consistent velocity but differs in pressure drop characteristics.

## Velocity Limitations and Considerations

Velocity selection balances competing objectives including pressure drop minimization, noise control, duct size economy, and erosion prevention. No single velocity suits all applications, requiring engineering judgment based on specific project requirements.

### Noise Generation Velocity Limits

Turbulence-induced noise generation increases dramatically as velocity exceeds certain thresholds, creating objectionable sound levels in occupied spaces. Supply duct velocities typically limit to 1200 to 2000 fpm for commercial installations, with lower velocities (800 to 1200 fpm) appropriate for sound-critical applications.

Return duct velocity limits generally permit 1000 to 1800 fpm for commercial systems, recognizing that return systems operate at lower pressure and generate less turbulent noise. Residential applications warrant substantially lower velocities, typically 600 to 900 fpm maximum.

Terminal velocity near air outlets critically affects occupant comfort and perceived noise. Final duct sections approaching diffusers or registers should reduce velocity to 500 to 800 fpm to minimize discharge noise and ensure acceptable throw characteristics.

### Erosion Velocity Considerations

Particulate-laden airstreams create abrasive wear at elevated velocities, particularly at elbows and other directional changes where particles impact duct surfaces. Industrial exhaust systems and outdoor air intakes in dusty environments warrant erosion velocity analysis.

Maximum recommended velocities for dust-laden air typically range from 2000 to 3500 fpm depending on particle characteristics, with lower values appropriate for hard, angular particles. Severe erosion conditions may necessitate abrasion-resistant materials or sacrificial wear surfaces.

## Supply, Return, and Outdoor Air Velocity Ranges

Supply duct main trunks typically operate at 1200 to 2000 fpm in commercial installations, balancing economy against noise generation. Branch ducts reduce to 800 to 1200 fpm, with final approach sections to terminals limiting to 500 to 800 fpm.

Return duct velocities permit similar or slightly lower ranges compared to supply systems, commonly 1000 to 1800 fpm for mains and 800 to 1200 fpm for branches. The lower pressure differential across return systems reduces noise generation potential at equivalent velocity.

Outdoor air duct velocities frequently operate higher than supply or return systems, commonly 1500 to 2500 fpm, recognizing that these ducts typically route through mechanical rooms or concealed spaces remote from occupied areas. Weather hood velocities should limit to 800 to 1200 fpm to prevent rain entrainment and excessive pressure drop.

## Cross-Sectional Area Optimization

Duct cross-sectional area establishes the fundamental relationship between airflow and velocity through the continuity equation Q = A × V, where Q represents airflow (CFM), A equals area (ft²), and V denotes velocity (fpm).

Minimum cross-sectional area for a given airflow and maximum velocity equals A = Q / V, establishing the baseline dimension requirement. Practical sizing typically rounds upward to standard dimension increments, slightly reducing velocity below the calculated maximum.

Area reductions at branch takeoffs follow systematic progressions that maintain velocity ranges within acceptable limits. Equal friction design maintains approximately constant pressure drop per unit length, requiring area reductions proportional to airflow reductions. Static regain design increases area more rapidly, reducing velocity to recover static pressure.
