Fossil fuel resources provide the majority of global primary energy and remain the dominant fuel source for building heating systems. Coal, natural gas, and petroleum products formed through geological processes over millions of years, converting organic matter into concentrated energy carriers. These fuels power furnaces, boilers, and combined heat and power systems while serving as feedstock for electricity generation supplying HVAC equipment.

## Formation and Classification

Fossil fuels originate from ancient organic matter subjected to heat and pressure in subsurface geological formations. The transformation process (diagenesis for coal, catagenesis for oil and gas) determines fuel properties including energy density, combustion characteristics, and impurity content.

**Coal** forms from terrestrial plant matter accumulated in swamp environments, progressively transformed through peat, lignite, subbituminous, bituminous, and anthracite ranks as temperature and pressure increase. Carbon content ranges from 25-30% (lignite) to 86-98% (anthracite), directly affecting heating value which spans 4,000-15,000 Btu/lb.

**Natural gas** primarily consists of methane generated from thermal decomposition of organic matter at depths typically exceeding 7,500 feet where temperatures surpass 200°F. Gas accumulates in porous reservoir rock sealed by impermeable cap rock or remains dissolved in crude oil (associated gas) until pressure reduction during production.

**Petroleum products** include fuel oils and liquefied petroleum gases (propane, butane) derived from crude oil refining. Fractional distillation separates crude oil into components based on boiling point, producing gasoline, kerosene, diesel, and residual fuel oils. Heating oils (No. 2 and No. 4-6) serve building heating applications, offering energy density of 130,000-150,000 Btu/gallon.

## Global Reserve Distribution

Fossil fuel reserves concentrate in specific geographic regions, creating supply dependencies and geopolitical considerations. Natural gas reserves total approximately 7,200 trillion cubic feet globally, with 40% in the Middle East and 30% in Russia/Central Asia. Coal reserves exceed 1 trillion short tons, concentrated in the United States (25%), Russia (17%), China (14%), and Australia (9%).

Reserve distribution influences long-term fuel availability and pricing for HVAC applications. North American natural gas abundance following the shale gas revolution improved supply security and reduced price volatility compared to imported petroleum products. Coal reserves remain vast but environmental concerns and emissions regulations limit new coal-fired heating system installations.

Reserve-to-production ratios indicate remaining supply duration at current consumption rates. Natural gas R/P ratios average 50 years globally but exceed 100 years in the Middle East while ranging 12-15 years in North America. Lower North American ratios reflect conservative reserve booking rather than imminent depletion, as continuous drilling replaces produced reserves.

## Extraction and Production Technologies

Modern extraction employs increasingly sophisticated technologies accessing progressively difficult resources. Conventional production targets permeable reservoirs with natural pressure drive, while unconventional methods unlock tight formations, shale deposits, and deep offshore accumulations.

Natural gas production transformed through horizontal drilling and hydraulic fracturing, creating artificial permeability in formations previously considered uneconomic. Multi-stage fracturing along horizontal laterals extending 5,000-10,000 feet maximizes reservoir contact, economically producing gas from shale with permeability measured in nanodarcies (billionths of a darcy).

Coal mining employs surface methods (strip mining, mountaintop removal) for shallow deposits and underground methods (room-and-pillar, longwall) for deeper seams. Surface mining dominates U.S. production, extracting 70% of coal output from Powder River Basin deposits with minimal overburden. Underground mining accesses deeper, higher-rank coals in Appalachian basins.

Enhanced oil recovery techniques including CO₂ flooding and steam injection extend petroleum field life, improving ultimate recovery from 30-40% (primary depletion) to 50-60%. These methods increase production costs but unlock substantial additional reserves as technology improves and prices justify application.

## Distribution Infrastructure

Fossil fuel distribution employs mode-specific infrastructure matching fuel physical properties and demand patterns. Natural gas pipelines form extensive networks operating at pressures from 500-1,400 psi (transmission) to 0.25-60 psi (distribution), delivering continuous supply to stationary equipment. Underground storage in depleted reservoirs and salt caverns provides seasonal balancing, critical for heating-dominated climates.

Coal transportation relies on unit trains (100+ hopper cars) hauling 10,000-15,000 tons from mines to power plants or industrial users. Barge and ocean vessel transport serve coastal facilities. On-site storage requirements range from 30-60 days supply, necessitating substantial space and material handling systems.

Fuel oil delivery employs tank trucks for residential and small commercial users, with larger facilities receiving deliveries via pipeline, barge, or rail tank car. On-site storage tanks sized for 7-30 days consumption buffer supply interruptions and enable bulk purchasing during favorable pricing periods.

## Combustion Properties and HVAC Applications

Fuel combustion properties determine burner design, air supply requirements, and emissions characteristics. Stoichiometric air requirements range from 10.0 ft³ air per ft³ gas (natural gas) to 13.7 lb air per lb fuel (bituminous coal), affecting draft requirements and combustion air delivery systems.

Natural gas burns cleanly with minimal particulate emissions, requiring no ash handling and producing combustion products primarily consisting of CO₂ and H₂O. Sulfur content below 5 ppm eliminates SO₂ emissions concerns. These characteristics favor gas-fired equipment for applications requiring clean combustion and minimal maintenance.

Fuel oils contain 0.5-2.5% sulfur (depending on grade and refinery processing), producing SO₂ emissions requiring flue gas treatment or stack height sufficient for dispersion in some jurisdictions. Heavier oils require heating to 180-220°F for proper atomization and combustion, adding system complexity.

Coal combustion produces substantial ash (5-40% by weight depending on rank and source), requiring ash handling systems, particulate control equipment, and ash disposal arrangements. Sulfur content ranging from 0.5-5% necessitates SO₂ control through low-sulfur coal selection, flue gas desulfurization, or emissions allowances. These factors largely eliminated coal from new commercial HVAC applications, though some existing industrial and institutional boiler plants continue coal firing.

## Environmental Considerations

Fossil fuel combustion produces CO₂ emissions contributing to atmospheric greenhouse gas concentrations. Emissions intensity varies substantially by fuel type:

| Fuel | CO₂ per MMBtu |
|------|---------------|
| Natural gas | 117 lb |
| No. 2 fuel oil | 161 lb |
| No. 6 fuel oil | 174 lb |
| Bituminous coal | 205 lb |

Natural gas's lower carbon-to-hydrogen ratio produces 40-50% less CO₂ per unit energy than coal, favoring gas for carbon emissions reduction. Particulate, SO₂, and NOx emissions similarly favor natural gas over coal and heavy fuel oils.

Methane leakage during natural gas production and distribution partially offsets CO₂ advantages, as methane exhibits 28-36 times greater global warming potential than CO₂ over 100-year timeframe. Industry efforts focus on leak detection and reduction to preserve gas's environmental benefits.

## Economic Factors and Fuel Selection

Delivered fuel costs drive HVAC system fuel selection alongside equipment capital costs, efficiency, and emissions requirements. Natural gas pricing exhibits regional variation based on pipeline access, ranging from $3-8/MMBtu for commercial users. Fuel oil pricing tracks crude oil markets plus refining and distribution margins, typically $12-20/MMBtu equivalent.

Equipment capital costs favor natural gas systems, as clean combustion eliminates ash handling, requires simpler controls, and produces compact equipment suitable for packaged products. Fuel oil systems require on-site storage tanks, leak detection, and spill containment, adding installed cost. Coal-fired systems demand extensive materials handling, ash removal, and emissions control, limiting application to large installations.

Long-term fuel supply contracts and pricing mechanisms affect operating cost projections. Natural gas utilities offer firm and interruptible service at different rates, with interruptible service providing lower prices in exchange for curtailment rights during peak demand. Fuel oil users may lock in prices through futures contracts, hedging price volatility risk.
